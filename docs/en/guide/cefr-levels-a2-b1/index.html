What do A2 and B1 mean? Find your language level and the right book

The letters you see on language courses, exams and book covers, such as A2 or B1, come from the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR). It splits language ability into six steps: A1 and A2 are basic, B1 and B2 independent, C1 and C2 proficient.

The steps in brief

  • A1: You can introduce yourself and understand very familiar everyday phrases.
  • A2: You understand and use short, simple sentences about daily life, if people speak slowly and clearly.
  • B1: You get the main points of texts on familiar topics and can describe an event or experience in connected sentences.
  • B2: You understand most details of complex texts and can talk comfortably with native speakers.
  • C1–C2: You follow long, demanding texts, implications and style, and use the language flexibly and almost flawlessly.

How to estimate your level

The quickest test is a real text. If every line has a word you don't know and the main idea keeps slipping away, that text is a step above you for now. If you can follow the story despite the odd unknown word, you are at the right level. If you read everything without stopping, move up a step.

What do A2 and B1 texts look like?

A2 texts use short sentences, mostly present and past tenses and everyday vocabulary. B1 texts join ideas with linking words, add vocabulary for feelings and opinions and keep more of the story's detail. The full text is the author's own words, with old-fashioned vocabulary, long sentences and style left intact.

Which level should you start classics at?

  • Start one step below your level: the first chapters introduce characters and setting, and reading comfortably keeps you going.
  • Start with a story you already know: knowing the plot makes the language easier.
  • Read with audio: seeing and hearing the same sentence trains pronunciation and listening together.
  • Drop a level when a scene gets hard and move up when it flows; a level is a tool, not a label.
